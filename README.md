# Endogenous-Radiolytic-Production-of-Oxygen-and-Its-Potential-to-Support-Habitable-Environments-in-Ocean-Worlds

This repository contains the report and slides from a project I completed at USTH for a course. I also presented this work at the 2025 SAGI Astrophysics Workshop.

**Abstract**: Europa, one of Jupiter’s largest moons, harbors a global ocean beneath its icy shell (Pappalardo et al., 1999; Kivelson et al., 2000) and is considered one of the most promising targets in the search for life beyond Earth. While the presence of liquid water is essential, life also requires access to chemical energy—particularly redox gradients—that drive metabolism. On Earth, microbial life thrives in deep subsurface ecosystems isolated from sunlight, sustained instead by chemical disequilibrium generated through radiolysis from the decay of radioactive elements (Lin et al., 2005, 2006). In such settings, ionizing radiation from the decay of isotopes like ⁴⁰K, ²³⁸U, ²³⁵U, and ²³²Th dissociates water molecules into hydrogen (H₂) and oxidants such as hydrogen peroxide (H₂O₂) and molecular oxygen (O₂). Here, we present a model to quantify O₂ production by water radiolysis in Europa’s subsurface ocean, focusing on gamma radiation from ⁴⁰K decay. We adopt potassium extraction factors based on geochemical modeling (Zolotov & Shock, 2001) to estimate the distribution of potassium in Europa's interior and, consequently, estimate the concentration of O₂ in the ocean. Our results show that, under the potassium distribution model of Zolotov & Shock (2001), ⁴⁰K-driven radiolysis can generate micromolar-level concentrations of O₂ in Europa’s ocean. This exceeds the minimum oxygen requirements for aerobic extremophiles on Earth, which are typically in the nanomolar range (~1.43 nM; Chu, 2016). These findings suggest that radiolysis may represent a significant and previously underappreciated energy source for potential life in Europa's ocean. Upcoming missions such as Europa Clipper will provide crucial data to test this hypothesis.
